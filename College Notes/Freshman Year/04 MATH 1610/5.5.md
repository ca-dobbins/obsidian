### U-Sub
How do we find $\int2x\sqrt{ 1+x^{2} }\,dx$? None of the rules we know apply here and no algebra will help. We'll use a substitution. Recall [[3.10#Differential|Differentials]].

$$
\begin{array}{l}
u=1+x^{2} \\
du=2x\,dx
\end{array}
$$

So,

$$
\int2x\sqrt{ 1+x^{2} }\,dx\to \int \sqrt{ 1+x^{2} }\,(2x\,dx)\to \int \sqrt{ u }\,du
$$

Which becomes $\frac{2}{3}u^{3/2}$. And now just substitute back in. 

$$
\frac{2}{3}u^{3/2}+C\to \frac{2}{3}(1+x^{2})^{3/2}+C
$$

***Def***: If $g(x)$ is differentiable with range $I$ and $f(x)$ is continuous on $I$, then if $u=g(x)$,

$$
\int f(g(x))g'(x)\,dx=f(g(x))
$$

The same substitution can work for [[5.2#Topic Definite Integrals|Definite Integrals]]. Recall that:

$$
\int \sqrt{ 2x+1 } \, dx =\frac{1}{3}(2x+1)^{3/2}+C
$$

So, if $\int \sqrt{ 2x+1 } \, dx$ equals that, then:

$$
\int_{0}^{4} \sqrt{ 2x+1 } \, dx =\frac{1}{3}(2x+1)^{3/2}|_{0}^{4}
$$

But there is a better way to do this. If we turn the integrand into a function of $u$ from the function of $x$, we should be able to change the limits of integration from values of $x$ into values of $u$.

$$
\int_{a}^{b} f(g(x))g'(x) \, dx =\int_{g(a)}^{g(b)} f(u) \, du 
$$

Where, $u=g(x)$ and $du=g'(x)$.

If $f(x)$ is odd, then $\int_{-a}^{a} f(x) \, dx=0$.
##### Example(s):
$\int4x^{3}\sin (x^4+3)\,dx$

$$
\begin{array}{l}
u=x^{4}+3 \\
du=4x^{3}\,dx
\end{array}
$$

$$
\int \sin(4x^{3}+3)(4x^{3}\,dx)\to \int \sin(u)\,du
$$

So then the integral equals $-\cos(u)+C$ and substituting back in:

$$
-\cos(4x^{3}+3)+C
$$

---
$\int x^{3}\sin(4x^{3}+3)\,dx$

$$
\begin{array}{l}
u=x^{4}+3 \\
du=4x^{3}\,dx \to \frac{1}{4}du=x^{3}\,dx
\end{array}
$$

$$
\int x^{3}\sin(4x^{3}+3)\,dx=-\frac{1}{4}\cos(4x^{3}+3)
$$

---
$$
\int_{0}^{4} \sqrt{ 2x+1 } \, dx =\int_{u(0)}^{u(4)} \frac{1}{2}\sqrt{ u } \, du 
$$

Where $u=2x+1$ and $du=2dx$. So the integral turns into:

$$
\int_{1}^{9} \frac{1}{2}\sqrt{ u } \, du 
$$
